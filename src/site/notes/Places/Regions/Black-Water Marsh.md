# Description
The air in the Black-Water Marsh smells of bitter peat and stagnant copper, hanging so thick it coats the back of your throat. Beneath a choked canopy of rotting cypress trees, the waist-deep water is completely opaque — an ink-black expanse of suffocating silt that swallows all light and muffles sound. Every step is a claustrophobic struggle against clinging mud, forcing you to wade blindly through glassy, unrippled water that effortlessly conceals sudden sinkholes, jagged roots, and whatever silent things glide just below.

## Seasonal Effects
### Spring — The Wet Season
The marsh floods constantly. The rain is heavy and persistent, turning the ground into a treacherous, sucking mire. The winds are moderate but get broken up by the dense, rotting canopy.

### Summer — The Suffocation
The air is entirely stagnant and punishingly hot. Rains are less frequent, but when they hit, they are violent, torrential thunderstorms that flash-flood the basin. The humidity is so high that sweat doesn't evaporate.

### Autumn — The Creeping Fog
The temperature drops, bringing a bone-deep, clammy chill. Rains turn into persistent, light drizzles. The defining feature is the massive cloud cover and thick, ground-level fog that reduces visibility to a few feet on most mornings.

### Winter — The Slush
True, solid freezes are rare because the rotting vegetation generates a faint heat, resulting in a landscape of freezing mud and sharp slush. The precipitation falls as freezing rain or sleet. The wind picks up, biting right through wet armour.
